Demo 3 — Minimum Viable Product

Progress on activated carbon filtration testing, updated housing design, ultrasonic overflow detection, live turbidity display, and continued system integration.

Overview

Demo 3 represents the Minimum Viable Product stage of Water Works. Since Demo 2, the project has shifted from mainly testing individual components to building a more complete and testable system. The biggest technical change is that we are now using activated carbon as the model contaminant being filtered out, instead of diatomaceous earth. This change gave us a more visible and measurable contaminant for evaluating the 20 µm filter and turbidity sensor.

The overall design goal remains the same: Water Works is an adaptive filtration system for irrigation that responds to changing water quality. Instead of filtering for a fixed amount of time, the system uses a turbidity sensor to monitor water clarity and recirculates water through the filter until it reaches a target level. Once the water is clean enough, the system can redirect it to a clean reservoir. This helps avoid unnecessary filtration while still reducing the risk of clogging in irrigation systems.

This demo focuses on three main areas: filtration performance, housing integration, and user/safety feedback. We developed an activated carbon standard curve, collected multi-pass filtration data through a 20 µm filter, redesigned the housing to better align the filter and sensor, added an ultrasonic sensor as an overflow/clogging safety feature, and added a display screen for live turbidity readings.

Key Change: Activated Carbon as the Model Contaminant

A major update for Demo 3 was switching the model contaminant from diatomaceous earth to activated carbon. In Demo 2, diatomaceous earth was difficult to filter consistently because much of its particle size distribution was too fine for our low-pressure filtration setup. This made it hard to produce repeatable turbidity changes, especially at lower concentrations.

Activated carbon works better for this stage because it produces a strong dark turbidity signal and is easier to visually track during filtration. Since the contaminant is darker, the turbidity sensor can more clearly detect the difference between dirty water and filtered water. This makes it easier to evaluate the effect of initial concentration, number of passes, and filter performance.

In this version of the project, activated carbon is not being used as the adsorbent in the filter. Instead, it is the suspended contaminant that we are trying to remove. This distinction is important because our current testing is focused on solid-liquid separation: removing suspended carbon particles from water using a 20 µm filter.

Why Activated Carbon Works Better for Testing

  • It produces a strong turbidity signal that the sensor can detect.
  • It creates a visible before/after difference in water clarity.
  • It works better with the 20 µm filter currently used in the prototype.
  • It allows us to test multiple initial concentrations and compare removal.
  • It makes multi-pass filtration trends easier to observe.

Application of Chemical Engineering Principles

Particle Size Distribution and Filtration

The effectiveness of filtration depends on the relationship between the filter pore size and the particle size distribution of the contaminant. In this demo, activated carbon particles are suspended in water and passed through a 20 µm filter. Particles larger than the effective pore size are trapped, while smaller particles may pass through.

This means that the filter does not remove “all carbon” equally. Its performance depends on the actual size range of the suspended activated carbon particles. If a large fraction of particles are above 20 µm, the filter should remove them effectively. If many particles are below that cutoff, they may remain suspended and still affect turbidity readings.

Filter Cutoff Spectrum

The 20 µm filter represents a middle ground between capture efficiency and flow rate. A coarser filter would allow more water to pass through easily, but would also allow more activated carbon to remain in the water. A much finer filter would remove more particles, but could create too much resistance for the peristaltic pump.

This tradeoff was a major lesson from earlier filtration testing. Very fine filters can theoretically remove more suspended material, but they may not be usable at ambient pressure. For this project, the goal is not only to maximize removal, but to maintain a flow rate that the system can realistically operate with.

Cake Filtration and Multi-Pass Dynamics

A key mechanism in the current system is cake filtration. During the first pass, activated carbon particles begin collecting on the filter surface. This trapped layer becomes a secondary filtering medium. As the cake layer grows, it reduces the effective pore size and helps capture additional particles in later passes.

This explains why the multi-pass filtration data improves with repeated cycles. The first few passes remove a large amount of suspended carbon, while later passes provide smaller improvements. Eventually, the system approaches the clean water baseline, and each additional pass gives diminishing returns.

Concentration Effects on Detection

The turbidity sensor does not directly “know” the concentration. It reads changes in light transmission through the water. At higher activated carbon concentrations, the water is darker and scatters or absorbs more light, giving a stronger sensor response. When filtration removes particles, the signal changes more clearly.

At lower concentrations, the absolute change in signal is smaller. This means the sensor is more affected by noise, mixing differences, and baseline variation. This is why low-concentration data can appear less smooth, especially after several passes when the water is already close to clean.

Pressure Drop and Flow Rate Tradeoff

Darcy’s Law helps explain the tradeoff between filtration efficiency and flow. As filter pore size decreases, or as particles build up on the filter, hydraulic resistance increases. This increases pressure drop across the filter and can reduce flow rate.

Because our system uses a peristaltic pump rather than a high-pressure pump, the filter cannot be too restrictive. The design challenge is finding a filter fine enough to remove activated carbon, but open enough to maintain useful flow. The 20 µm filter gives a workable balance for the current prototype.

Feedback Control Loop

The long-term control strategy is based on a closed-loop system. The turbidity sensor reads water clarity, the Arduino compares the reading to a threshold, and the system decides whether to continue recirculating or divert water to the clean reservoir.

This is the core “adaptive” part of Water Works. Instead of filtering for a fixed time, the system adjusts based on actual water quality. The activated carbon data helps us understand what sensor readings correspond to cleaner water and where a useful threshold could be placed.

Hardware Progress: Pinch Valve Flow Diversion

The pinch valve remains a central part of the adaptive filtration design. The purpose of the valve is to redirect flow based on the turbidity sensor reading. If the water is still too turbid, the valve should keep water in the recirculation loop. Once the reading reaches the target clarity threshold, the valve can redirect water toward the clean reservoir.

The pinch valve is useful because it does not place mechanical parts directly in the water. Instead, it pinches flexible tubing from the outside. This keeps the water path cleaner and makes the valve easier to integrate into the system.

Hardware Progress: Ultrasonic Sensor Safety Feature

An ultrasonic sensor was added above the filter as a safety feature. If the filter begins to clog, water may start backing up above the filter. The ultrasonic sensor can monitor this water level in real time and alert the user before overflow occurs.

This gives the system a way to detect a physical failure condition, not just water quality. Since clogging is one of the major risks in filtration systems, the ultrasonic sensor makes the prototype more robust and safer during operation.

Activated Carbon Calibration: Standard Curve

Before testing filtration performance, we created a standard curve using known activated carbon concentrations. These measurements were taken with no filter. The goal was to relate activated carbon concentration to turbidity sensor reading before introducing filtration.

This standard curve helps interpret the filtration data because it confirms that the sensor responds to changes in activated carbon concentration. The relationship is approximately linear over much of the tested range, although the highest concentration points appear to level off, suggesting some sensor saturation or measurement limits at high turbidity.

Activated Carbon Filtration Testing

After building the standard curve, we tested activated carbon removal using the 20 µm filter. Three initial concentrations were tested: 0.01, 0.02, and 0.03. Each sample was passed through the filter multiple times, and turbidity sensor readings were recorded after 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 passes.

Clean water was measured separately and gave a baseline reading of -0.09775. As activated carbon is removed, the turbidity reading moves closer to this clean water value.

Results and Interpretation

The filtration data shows that turbidity decreases with additional filtration passes. For all three initial concentrations, the readings move closer to the clean water baseline after filtration. The largest improvement occurs in the first 2–3 passes, which shows that the filter removes most of the activated carbon early in the process.

After 2–3 passes, the improvement begins to level off. This is consistent with diminishing returns: once most of the suspended activated carbon has been removed, each additional pass produces a smaller change in turbidity. This supports the adaptive design because the system should not run longer than necessary once the water is already close to clean.

Some variability appears in the data. The 0.02 concentration reached slightly beyond the clean water baseline after four passes, which is not physically meaningful and is likely due to sensor noise, baseline drift, or mixing differences. The 0.01 concentration also showed a small decrease in apparent performance at the fourth pass. Additional trials will be needed to add error bars and improve confidence in the final results.

Key Finding

The main finding from Demo 3 is that activated carbon can be measurably removed using the 20 µm filter, and most of the removal happens in the first few passes. The system shows strong improvement by 2–3 passes, then begins to level off. This supports the main adaptive filtration idea: the system should use sensor feedback to determine when filtration is complete instead of relying on a fixed run time.

Project Management + Next Steps

Action items before the final demo, in rough priority order.

Integration

Finalize the physical housing assembly. This includes mounting the filter holder, intermediate tank, turbidity sensor, tubing, pump, and electronics into the housing. Leak testing is also needed to confirm that water moves consistently through the intended flow path.

Control

Complete pinch valve automation. The final system should connect the turbidity threshold to the valve position so that water can switch automatically between recirculation and clean output.

Testing

Repeat activated carbon filtration trials. More runs at each concentration will allow us to add error bars and improve confidence in the percent removal data.

Sensor

Refine the live turbidity display. The screen should clearly show turbidity readings and eventually system status, such as filtering, clean, or overflow warning.

Safety

Test ultrasonic overflow detection. The sensor needs to reliably detect when water begins backing up above the filter so that it can warn the user before overflow occurs.

Future Sensor Expansion

Add pH or TDS sensing if time allows. These would provide additional water quality information alongside turbidity.

Final Takeaway

Demo 3 shows that Water Works is moving from separate working parts toward a full adaptive filtration prototype. The activated carbon testing gives us usable sensor data, the 20 µm filter shows clear removal over repeated passes, and the updated housing, ultrasonic sensor, and display screen make the system more functional and user-facing. The remaining work is focused on repeatability, final integration, and validating the complete system for the final demonstration.
