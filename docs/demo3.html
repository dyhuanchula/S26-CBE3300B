Demo 3 — Minimum Viable Product

Progress on activated carbon filtration testing, sensor calibration, housing integration, ultrasonic overflow sensing, and live turbidity display.

Overview

Demo 3 marks the next stage of development from Demo 2, moving from an intermediate prototype toward a more complete and testable minimum viable product. The main updates since Demo 2 are the switch from diatomaceous earth to activated carbon as the model contaminant being filtered out, the development of a new activated carbon turbidity calibration curve, multi-pass filtration testing through a 20 µm filter, and continued progress on housing and sensor integration.

The overall system goal remains the same: Water Works is an adaptive filtration system for irrigation that responds to changing water quality. Instead of running at a constant setting, the system uses a turbidity sensor to monitor water clarity and cycles water through the filter until it reaches a target level. Once that threshold is met, the system can redirect the water to a clean reservoir. This helps reduce unnecessary filtration while still preventing clogging in irrigation systems.

This demo focuses especially on proving that our sensor and filter setup can detect and remove a suspended contaminant over repeated passes. Activated carbon was used as the contaminant because it creates a strong turbidity signal and can be visibly and quantitatively tracked during filtration. The current results show that most filtration improvement happens in the first 2–3 passes, after which the system begins approaching the clean water baseline.

Key Change: Activated Carbon as the Model Contaminant

A major update for Demo 3 was switching the model contaminant from diatomaceous earth to activated carbon. In Demo 2, diatomaceous earth was difficult to filter consistently because much of the particle size distribution was too fine for our low-pressure, mesh-based filtration setup. This made it hard to produce reliable turbidity changes and made the filtration results less repeatable.

Activated carbon is now being used as the contaminant that we are filtering out. This is important because activated carbon creates a strong dark turbidity signal in water and can be tracked more clearly by the turbidity sensor. As the water passes through the 20 µm filter, activated carbon particles are removed, and the sensor reading moves closer to the clean water baseline.

This change made our filtration testing more useful because it allowed us to compare initial concentration, number of passes, and percent removal. It also gave us a clearer way to demonstrate the value of multi-pass recirculation in our system.

Why Activated Carbon Works Better for This Stage

  • Produces a strong turbidity signal that the sensor can detect.
  • Creates a visible before/after difference during filtration.
  • Works better with the 20 µm filter currently used in our system.
  • Allows comparison across multiple concentrations and pass counts.
  • Makes it easier to show diminishing returns after repeated filtration.

Application of ChemE Principles

Particle Size Distribution and Filtration

The effectiveness of the filter is governed by the relationship between the filter pore size and the particle size distribution of the contaminant. In this demo, activated carbon particles are suspended in water and passed through a 20 µm filter. Particles larger than the effective pore size are captured, while smaller particles may pass through. This means filtration performance depends on both the pore size of the filter and the actual size range of the activated carbon particles in the suspension.

Filter Cutoff Spectrum

The 20 µm filter represents a practical balance between filtration efficiency and usable flow rate. A filter that is too coarse would allow too much activated carbon to pass through, while a filter that is too fine would create too much resistance for the peristaltic pump. Based on our earlier testing, very fine filtration media can block flow at ambient pressure. The 20 µm filter is fine enough to show measurable removal while still allowing water to move through the system.

Cake Filtration and Multi-Pass Dynamics

A key mechanism in our current setup is cake filtration. During the first pass, activated carbon particles begin collecting on the filter surface. This trapped layer becomes a secondary filter, reducing the effective pore size and helping capture additional particles on later passes. This explains why multi-pass recirculation improves filtration performance even when the same filter is used each time.

The data also shows diminishing returns. Most of the removal happens early, especially in the first 2–3 passes. After that, the water approaches the clean water baseline, so each additional pass gives a smaller improvement.

Concentration Effects on Detection

The turbidity sensor's ability to detect a change depends on both the starting concentration of activated carbon and the absolute change in sensor reading after filtration. At higher concentrations, the water is darker and the sensor signal changes more clearly as particles are removed. At lower concentrations, the change is smaller and can be closer to the noise floor of the sensor. This affects how reliably the system can decide whether water is clean enough to stop recirculating.

Pressure Drop and Flow Rate Tradeoff

Darcy’s Law helps explain the tradeoff between filtration and flow. As pore size decreases or particles build up on the filter, hydraulic resistance increases. This can reduce flow rate and increase the load on the pump. Our system uses a low-pressure peristaltic pump, so the filter cannot be too restrictive. The design challenge is finding a filter that removes enough activated carbon to improve clarity while still allowing useful flow.

Feedback Control Loop

The long-term control strategy reads turbidity, compares it to a target threshold, and uses that signal to decide whether water should continue recirculating or be diverted toward the clean reservoir. This is the core adaptive behavior of Water Works. The activated carbon testing helps define the sensor readings needed to make this control loop meaningful and reliable.

Hardware Progress: Pinch Valve Flow Diversion

The pinch valve remains a central part of the adaptive filtration system. Its purpose is to redirect water based on turbidity. If the water is still too turbid, the system should continue recirculating it through the filter. Once the turbidity sensor indicates that the water has reached the desired clarity, the valve can redirect the flow toward the clean reservoir.

The pinch valve design is useful because it keeps the mechanical components outside the water path. Instead of placing a moving part directly in the water, the valve pinches flexible tubing from the outside. This reduces contamination risk and keeps the fluid path simpler.

Hardware Progress: Ultrasonic Sensor Safety Feature

An ultrasonic sensor was added above the filter as a safety feature. If the filter begins to clog, water may start backing up above the filter. The ultrasonic sensor can monitor this water level in real time and alert the user before overflow occurs.

This is important because clogging is one of the main risks in any filtration system. The turbidity sensor tells us about water clarity, but the ultrasonic sensor gives the system a way to detect a physical failure condition. This makes the prototype safer and more realistic for use without constant supervision.

Activated Carbon Filtration Testing

After building the standard curve, we tested activated carbon removal through the 20 µm filter. Three initial concentrations were tested: 0.01, 0.02, and 0.03. Each sample was passed through the filter multiple times, and the turbidity sensor reading was recorded after each pass.

Clean water was measured separately and gave a baseline reading of -0.09775. As filtration removes activated carbon, the readings move closer to this clean water value.

Results and Interpretation

The filtration data shows that turbidity decreases with additional filtration passes. For all three initial concentrations, the readings move closer to the clean water baseline after the first pass and continue improving through later passes. Most of the improvement occurs within the first 2–3 passes.

This trend supports the cake filtration explanation. As activated carbon collects on the filter, the filter becomes more effective at catching additional particles. However, the gains decrease over time as the sample approaches the clean water baseline. The 0.02 trial slightly passed the clean water baseline after four passes, which is likely due to sensor noise, baseline variation, or mixing inconsistency. The 0.01 trial also showed some variability at the fourth pass. These results show that more trials and error bars will be needed for the final report.

Key Finding

The key finding from Demo 3 is that activated carbon can be measurably removed using the 20 µm filter, and most of the removal occurs in the first few passes. This supports the adaptive filtration concept because the system should not run for a fixed time. Instead, it should use turbidity feedback to determine when additional filtration no longer provides meaningful improvement.

Final Takeaway
