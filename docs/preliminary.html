Preliminary Design

Initial design documentation: market context, overall concept, core principles, parts list, and prototype plan.

1) Introduction (Market Context)

Our goal is to build an “adaptive Brita”-style purifier that continuously recirculates water through a particulate filter and an adsorbent bed until quality metrics meet a threshold, then dispenses treated water. The device targets low-cost, user-friendly purification for everyday use where customers care about clarity, taste/odor, and basic quality feedback.

  • Who is this for? (e.g., dorms, renters, office kitchens, emergency backup)
  • What problem? (e.g., inconsistent taste/odor, particulate/turbidity, user confidence)
  • Why adaptive? Real-time sensing enables automatic “keep cycling until clean” behavior and maintenance alerts.

2) Overall Design

Our purifier is designed to be connected to a continuous water source (e.g., a faucet adapter, jug with a feed line, or other constant supply). The system operates in a semi-batch cycle: it produces a batch of clean water on demand whenever the clean reservoir is low, rather than filtering continuously at all times. This keeps the design simple, limits wasted pumping, and makes the “adaptive” behavior easy to enforce—keep filtering until the sensor says it’s clean.

System Concept (Semi-Batch, Sensor-Gated)

  • Source connection: A feed line supplies raw water to the system when a fill valve is opened.
  • Batch treatment chamber: A dedicated “raw batch” container is filled to a set level each cycle.
  • Recirculation loop: A pump drives water through a turbidity sensing section and a filter module repeatedly.
  • Clean reservoir: Once clean, water is routed into a final reservoir where pH is measured and displayed.
  • User interaction: The user empties the clean reservoir whenever they want; the system detects “low/empty” and starts the next batch.

Flow Path (Detailed)

  • 1) Fill: When the clean reservoir is detected as low, the controller opens the inlet valve to fill the raw batch chamber to a target level (level sensing via ultrasonic sensor or a float switch).
  • 2) Recirculate + Filter: The pump circulates water through: (i) an optical turbidity sensing section, then (ii) the filtration module (particulate filter, and/or carbon stage depending on your final build), returning to the raw batch chamber. This repeats until the turbidity measurement indicates the water is sufficiently clear.
  • 3) “Pass” Condition: When turbidity stays below a threshold for a hold time (plus hysteresis), the controller switches the valve state from recirculation to transfer.
  • 4) Transfer to Clean Reservoir: Two pinch valves reconfigure the plumbing: they close the recirculation return path and open the path to the clean reservoir, sending the treated batch into the clean container.
  • 5) Display + Ready State: A pH sensor in the clean reservoir provides a real-time pH readout on a screen. The system remains idle until the clean reservoir is emptied or drops below a low threshold, at which point the cycle repeats.

Turbidity Sensing with a Photoresistor (Optical Clarity)

Our “adaptive” decision variable is turbidity/clarity measured optically. A low-cost method uses an LED light source and a photoresistor (LDR) arranged across a small inline chamber (or a transparent segment of tubing). Suspended particles scatter and absorb light, so cloudy water reduces the light reaching the photoresistor. As filtration improves clarity, more light reaches the sensor and the photoresistor’s resistance changes accordingly.

  • Measurement idea: The microcontroller reads a voltage divider output from the LDR; this value correlates with transmitted light intensity.
  • Why it works here: Even if it’s not a lab-grade NTU meter, it provides a consistent relative signal to detect “getting clearer” and “clear enough.”
  • Practical design notes: Use a light-shielded housing (to block ambient light), consistent geometry, and averaging/median filtering to reduce noise.
  • Calibration plan: Record a “clean baseline” (clear water) and a “dirty baseline” (intentional turbidity, e.g., a small amount of fine silt), then set thresholds with hysteresis (different enter/exit thresholds) to prevent valve-chatter.

Control Logic (High-Level)

  • Trigger: If clean reservoir level is low → start a new batch.
  • Fill: Open inlet valve until batch chamber reaches set level.
  • Filter loop: Pump ON; read turbidity continuously; keep recirculating while above threshold.
  • Pass + transfer: If turbidity remains below threshold for N seconds → switch pinch valves to route flow to clean reservoir.
  • Stop: When batch chamber is empty or clean reservoir is full → stop pump and return to idle.
  • Display: pH is measured in the clean reservoir and shown on the screen (informational + diagnostic).

3) Chemical / Physical Principles

Our adaptive purifier uses physical filtration, chemical adsorption, and sensor-based feedback to determine when water is clean enough to dispense.

Particulate removal and turbidity

Suspended particles such as silt, organic debris, and microorganisms scatter light and make water appear cloudy. This is measured as turbidity. A mesh and filter-pad stage removes these particles through size exclusion and interception. As particles are removed, light scattering decreases and clarity increases, allowing turbidity to be used as a real-time indicator of filtration progress.

Adsorption using activated carbon

After particulates are removed, water flows through a packed bed of activated carbon. The carbon’s high surface area adsorbs dissolved organic compounds, chlorine, and odor-causing molecules through surface interactions within its pores. This improves taste and smell even when the water already looks clear.

pH as a chemical indicator

pH measures hydrogen ion concentration and provides information about water chemistry and safety. Drinking water is typically acceptable between pH 6.5 and 8.5. Large deviations can indicate contamination, corrosion, or improper treatment. While pH is not the main “clean/not clean” trigger, it provides useful diagnostic feedback.

Water level and flow control

An ultrasonic sensor measures the distance to the water surface to estimate tank level. This enables automatic refilling, prevents dry-running the pump, and stops dispensing when the clean tank is full.

5) In Depth: Turbidity (Optical Clarity)

Turbidity is our primary “adaptive” signal because particle filtration directly changes optical clarity in real time. We can implement an inline LED + photodiode/phototransistor sensor or a turbidity module.

  • Placement: after filter/adsorbent in the recirculation loop to measure improvement.
  • Algorithm: recirculate until clarity > threshold for N seconds (hold time) + hysteresis.
  • Calibration: define clean baseline vs intentionally cloudy baseline (e.g., fine silt test).

Include a simple plot later: sensor reading vs time during a cleaning cycle.

6) In Depth: pH

The pH sensor provides a real-time readout for user awareness and logging. In this design, pH is not used as the main “clean/not clean” gate, but it is useful for diagnostics and reporting.

  • Response time target: stable reading within ~30–60 seconds in a well-mixed chamber.
  • Placement: in the chamber or in a flowing side-stream to avoid stagnant readings.
  • Calibration: 2-point calibration (pH 4 and pH 7 buffers; optionally pH 10).

7) In Depth: Adsorbent

We plan to use a DIY packed-bed adsorbent module to reduce chlorine/taste/odor and some organic compounds (depending on adsorbent selection). The bed will be held between mesh + filter pads to retain granules and block fines.

  • Candidate media: activated carbon (or catalytic carbon if chloramine is a focus).
  • Design variables: bed length/diameter, packing density, flow rate (contact time).
  • Feasibility calc: estimate contact time = (bed volume / flow) and expected media life (later).

8) In Depth: Ultrasound Sensor (Water Level)

The ultrasonic sensor measures the distance to the water surface to estimate chamber or reservoir level. This enables auto-refill behavior and prevents dry-running the pump.

  • Mounting: above the water, pointed downward; shield from splashing.
  • Noise sources: bubbles, turbulence, angled surfaces → use averaging/median filtering.
  • Fallback option: float switch for simple low/high water detection.

10) Future Considerations + Additions (If Time)

While the current system focuses on turbidity-based control, activated carbon adsorption, and pH monitoring, several upgrades could improve accuracy, automation, and real-world usability.

Expanded water quality sensing

Adding a conductivity or total dissolved solids (TDS) sensor would allow the system to quantify dissolved contaminants that are not detected by turbidity alone. This would provide a second chemical check after carbon filtration and improve confidence in water quality.

Automatic pH control

The pH sensor could be paired with small dosing pumps to automatically adjust acidity, keeping the water in a range that is optimal for adsorption and safe for consumption. This would reduce manual intervention and improve consistency across different water sources.

Data logging and user interface

Future versions could include wireless data logging or SD card storage to track turbidity, pH, and filtration cycles over time. This would allow users to monitor filter performance and know when maintenance or replacement is needed.

Scalability and cartridge design

The activated carbon bed could be redesigned as a replaceable cartridge, making the system easier to maintain and scale for household or portable applications.
